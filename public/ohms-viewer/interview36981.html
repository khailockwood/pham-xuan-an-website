00:05:24 - American Ideology, Foreign Intervention, and Vietnamese Resistance in early Vietnam War

Play segment

Direct segment link:
interview36981.html#segment324

Partial Transcript: What was your role during the war? During the American war after the French had left, and America had come in

Segment Synopsis: An begins by recounting the early stages of American involvement in the Indochinese conflict. An explained how South Vietnam went from French-backed to American-backed. Pham Xuan An, through his work with the American military training mission, explains one of the motivations of US intervention in Vietnam: U.S. ideology of self-determination of colonized countries and Wilsonian idealism, which it has applied in other areas around the world after World War 2. However, these ideals contrasted with colonial realities and Stalinist Soviet politics. Vietnamese nationalists felt trapped in the paradox of American and Soviet support, each vying for control. While unable to react to the Soviet contradiction, they were able to lead protests towards American intervention, leading to a turning point in 1950 toward resisting both French and American intervention.

Keywords: Edward Lansdale; Joseph Stalin; MAAG; Military Assistance Advisory Group; Ngo Dinh Diem; Nikita Khrushchev; Soviet Union; USSR; WW2; Wilsonian idealism; World War 2; Wilsonianism

00:12:11 - The Vietnam War v.s. Previous 20th Century Conflicts

Play segment

Direct segment link:
interview36981.html#segment731

Partial Transcript: What made the American—Vietnam War different than World War One, World War Two, as far as fighting it?

Segment Synopsis: The dialogue discusses why the Vietnam War differed from earlier U.S. wars. Pham Xuan An explains that anti-colonial wars in Vietnam were supported by China and the Soviet Union, creating a “war of liberation” that the U.S. had to confront, unlike previous colonial conflicts where America had limited involvement. While it appeared the U.S. repeated France’s mistakes, An argues circumstances were different: France was compelled to accept Vietnamese independence for financial and strategic reasons because of its unfortunate position during WWII, while post-WWII Americans faced a new global context. The U.S. could not allow European powers to regain influence in Asia, leading to a politically driven intervention that set a precedent for later conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan.

Keywords: Afghanistan; China; France; Germany; Iraq; Japan; Russia; Soviet Union; USSR; WWII; War of Liberation; World War I; World War II; WWI

00:17:07 - Post–Cold War U.S. Interventions, Oil Politics, and the Use of Pretexts

Play segment

Direct segment link:
interview36981.html#segment1027

Partial Transcript: Still dealing with the after-effects of the collapse of communist—the Soviet Union.

Segment Synopsis: Pham Xuan An argues that after the collapse of the Soviet Union, the United States continued frequent military interventions driven by long-term geopolitical and economic interests, particularly control over oil. He describes a pattern of repeated wars that are costly, destabilizing, and difficult to reconstruct, often justified through political pretexts rather than transparent causes. An links interventions in the Middle East, and Central Asia to fears of rival powers such as Russia and China gaining influence as well as the inability to shift from Cold War mindsets. He compares Iraq to Vietnam, emphasizing that while tactics may differ, U.S. strategy remains shaped by politics, resource security, and the strategic construction of justifications for war.
McMorris then asks why the Vietnam war was such an important turning point in American history, and to that, An responded that the war on terror is different than the earlier Cold War against the USSR because it is not a war against communism but a war against theory, ideology, and politics of the Arab World.

00:25:59 - Vietnam and the US in the Cold War Context

Play segment

Direct segment link:
interview36981.html#segment1559

Partial Transcript: What fascinates me is how Americans cannot forget the Vietnam War. It was such an important time period in American history that one person told me, not only did it—they said, it changed America as much as it changed Vietnam, and the war was fought on Vietnamese soil.

Segment Synopsis: McMorris suggests that South Vietnam may have “won” the war since present-day Vietnam is moving toward a free-market economy and loosening state control. Pham Xuan An disagrees, arguing that Vietnam’s true objective was national independence, not ideological victory. That independence, however, came with hard lessons: reliance on the Soviet Union and China weakened Vietnam’s position at the Geneva Conference. By the Paris Peace Talks, North Vietnam therefore sought to negotiate directly with the United States and minimize external influence. An situates these talks within America’s Wilsonian doctrine of self-determination, which guided U.S. global strategy after both world wars and shaped institutions such as the UN, IMF, World Bank, and GATT. This vision collapsed due to the Cold War after 1947, leading the U.S. to contain communism through Southeast Asian alliances, intelligence operations, and regional proxies/interventions. Meanwhile, instability in South Vietnam undermined U.S. efforts, but America successfully set up ASEAN with South Vietnam under the Thieu regime. Facing pressure from both war and communist allies, North Vietnam launched the Tet Offensive to force negotiations, successfully pushing the U.S. toward talks.

Keywords: China; Cold War; Nguyen Van Thieu; Nguyễn Văn Thiệu; North Vietnam; South Vietnam; Southeast Asia; Soviet Union; USSR; United States of America; Wilsonianism; independence; negotiations; political pressure; Russia

Segment Synopsis: McMorris asks about the freedom of press in Vietnam during the 1980s, to which An emphasizes that understanding Vietnam requires broad and sustained study. He notes that many books, both Vietnamese and international, now examine the war from multiple perspectives. An highlights the importance of scholarly research institutions in the United States, particularly a Vietnam-focused center at Texas Tech University. He explains that this institution, led by James R. Reckner with Douglas Pike as co-director, became a major repository for Vietnam War research after Pike transferred his extensive archives from UC Berkeley due to funding limitations. The center houses tens of thousands of books and documents from diverse international sources, including the Soviet Union, China, France, and Britain. An strongly recommends it as a valuable resource for serious research. McMorris adds that the center continues to expand its collection by gathering personal materials such as letters from war veterans, preserving a comprehensive historical record of the war.

00:42:50 - Vietnam's Adjustment to State-Controlled Market Economy

Direct segment link:
interview36981.html#segment2570

Partial Transcript: So back to your story. So that's why you want to understand this side. You talked to those who really know because there are many of them, they think they know, but they don't know.

Segment Synopsis: An expresses his concern over the people who lack a visionary view. He goes on to explain that there are two types of policies in the US: pragmatic and visionary policies. After the war, Vietnam struggled to transition to a market economy. Former capitalist elites can't be used in the new market economy era because they lacked knowledge of modern capitalism and were persecuted, while younger generations were unprepared for globalization. Many former soldiers were disappointed when socialist ideals were not realized. An maintains that postwar policies like re-education camps were unavoidable within a communist system. He expresses no personal regrets, viewing the war as a necessary historical struggle for Vietnamese independence, charaterized by difficult but inevitable adjustments to new realities.

Keywords: Adapting; Capitalism; Generational differences; Market economy; Pragmatic policy; Visionary policy
